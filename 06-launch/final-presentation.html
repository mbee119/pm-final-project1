Competitive Analysis & Journey Map

Responses

  • Role, who are you solving for? (the specific user segment or profile): Frontline delivery drivers who complete dozens of stops a day and need to confirm each one fast, represented by Diego.”
  • Goal, what is this user ultimately trying to achieve?: To complete every stop on his route quickly and reliably, and let dispatch know it’s done, so he can finish his shift on time without relying on workarounds.”
  • Friction, the main barrier (moment of misery) stopping them from succeeding: On every stop, a driver has to work through a slow, buried interface to record a simple fact, that the delivery is done. They skip the app and use a workaround instead: texting dispatch, keeping paper manifests, or calling the office.

The main barrier: the core actions, confirming a delivery and starting a route, take too many screens and sit too deep in the app. They are used dozens of times a day, at the moments when drivers can least afford to stop and navigate.

The moment of misery: Diego has just handed over a package, with his hands full and the clock running, and the app asks for three screens of taps to confirm it (UXR-01, BUG-2055). Starting the route has the same problem: the button a driver uses 30 times a day is buried under features he has never touched (UXR-11, BUG-2079).

Why it stops them succeeding: drivers’ goal is to complete stops and keep dispatch informed. The slow flow pushes them off the platform, so the app’s record of the day is wrong, and the dispatcher’s board goes stale.

Evidence strength: the three-screen flow is confirmed by both a user quote and a bug report (UXR-01, BUG-2055), and all seven focus group drivers put core-action speed first (UXR-12).

  • External tools, the outside platforms or tools the user is forced to use: For Diego, the research names exactly one external tool: text messaging to his dispatcher.
  • The process, the 3 to 5 manual steps the user takes to get the job done: 1. Hand over the package at the doorstep. The delivery itself is complete, but the app still shows the stop as open.

2. Decide to skip the app. Facing three screens of taps with a package in one hand and rain falling, he doesn’t start the Mark Delivered flow (evidenced, UXR-01).

3. Text the dispatcher that the stop is done. He composes a message identifying the stop, likely from memory or by glancing at the stop list (message format is assumed).

4. Move on to the next stop. The text is his only record of completion, so the app’s status for that stop stays unchanged.

5. Dispatcher reconciles it by hand. The dispatcher reads the text and relays or updates the board manually, which is where the status lag and trust issues in UXR-09 and BUG-2072 come from. This step happens on the dispatcher’s side, not Diego’s.

  • Core frustration, the exact moment the process feels most “broken”: The most broken moment is step 2: just after handing over the package, when Diego has to record the delivery and the app asks for three screens of taps. His hands are full and the clock is running, and all he needs to say is that the stop is done. This is the point where he gives up on the app and texts his dispatcher instead (UXR-01, BUG-2055).

This is the most broken point because every later step follows from it. The text, the unchanged status in the app and the dispatcher’s manual catch-up all happen only because he chose to skip the app here. It is also where the mismatch is sharpest: a task that should take a second is built as a multi-screen flow, at a moment when he has the least capacity for it. It is the clearest observable point in the research where the platform loses the job to a text message.

  • The evidence, a specific quote or behavior from the research that proves this: The strongest evidence is Diego’s own words in UXR-01:

“To mark a stop delivered I tap through three screens. In the rain, at a doorstep, with a package in one hand. I’ve started just texting my dispatcher instead.”

This one quote proves all three parts of the frustration. It names the friction (three screens to complete one action), the moment (a doorstep, with a package in hand) and the behavior (he has abandoned the app for a text message).

Roadmap, PRD & Prototype

Feature Roadmap, Module 4 · RouteLogic Velocity

Team: 2 engineers + 1 designer + 1 CS lead

Strategic anchors

  • Persona: Frontline Delivery Drivers
  • Primary metric: In-app completion rate (tops marked complete in the app ÷ total completed stops (in-app + text).)
  • Moment of misery: Drivers don’t trust the app enough to rely on it alone, so they carry paper as insurance. What they ask for is not more features but faster, more dependable core actions.
  • Guardrail: Weekly use of the specialized enterprise features (eg: reporting, AI predictive ETA)

Scoring

Feature · Value · Effort · Quadrant · Decision · Rationale

B1 One-Click Compliance Checklist · 5 · 2 · Quick Win · Now · Removes the 14.6-min step that pushes drivers back to paper and text. It is the most direct lever on in-app completion rate, scoped to pre-fill only so it can ship in time to be measured.

B2 Smart Daily Report Auto-Fill · 3 · 4 · Time Sinker · Cut · Serves the reporting guardrail but not the driver’s core friction. AI scope cannot be delivered or measured in 4 weeks, so we are not spending pilot capacity on it.

B3 Shift Handoff Wizard · 3 · 3 · Time Sinker · Later · A credible time saving (~6.8 min/day) that sits outside the stop-completion flow. Revisit once core actions are dependable.

B4 Mobile-First Coordinator Dashboard · 2 · 5 · Time Sinker · Cut · Targets coordinators, not our driver persona, and is the largest build on the list. It does not address why drivers keep paper.

B5 Step Progress Indicator · 2 · 1 · Fill-In · Next · Low-cost polish that improves orientation but won’t move completion rate in the pilot window. Only if there is spare capacity

B6 Driver Alert Notifications · 3 · 4 · Time Sinker · Later · Reliable alerts build the trust that replaces paper, but that trust accrues over weeks and the build exceeds the pilot. Start post-pilot with compliance-flag alerts, which reinforce B1.

B7 Contextual AI ETA Display · 3 · 2 · Fill-In · Next · Protects the enterprise-feature guardrail at low cost by putting an existing capability (11% adoption) in front of drivers. It stays passive so it doesn’t add friction to core actions.

B8 Fleet Analytics Manager View · 1 · 5 · Time Sinker · Cut · An executive and Sales-driven feature with no driver benefit and the highest cost. It does not serve the Velocity objective.

B9 Compliance Audit Trail Export · 2 · 3 · Time Sinker · Later · Serves auditors, not drivers. It becomes a low-cost by-product once B1 produces structured compliance data.

B10 In-App Coordinator Training · 1 · 3 · Time Sinker · Cut · Aimed at the wrong persona and unrelated to driver trust or speed, so it falls outside the initiative’s scope.

GTM Launch Plan & Success Dashboard

GTM Launch Plan, RouteLogic (B2B)

Field · Value

Feature · One-Click Compliance Checklist

Goal · Engagement

Launch tier · M, Targeted

Goal & Audience

  • Goal: Engagement, Goal: deepen adoption. Drivers already use RouteLogic heavily but skip the multi-screen flow at the moment of delivery and text dispatch instead. Behaviour change is our bottleneck, so we will measure adoption through in-app completion rate (stops completed in the app ÷ all completed stops) and aim for drivers to choose, and keep choosing, the one-click compliance checklist across pilot weeks 2 to 4.
  • Target audience: Frontline delivery drivers

Launch Tier

  • M, Targeted, The reach is small and known (drivers and dispatchers at 3 pilot accounts), so targeted comms is enough and keeps the group controlled. The potential revenue impact is significant, but it is not yet proven. As our largest account is trialing a competitor because RouteLogic is too slow at the point of delivery, this pilot is crucial. If drivers adopt the faster flow and daily frontline usage follows,; our premium reporting and compliance features may become worth paying for.

A silence would mean drivers will continue with their habit of carrying paper and text and may not notice a quiet change. Adoption stays low and the pilot looks like a failure when the real cause was that nobody knew. Dispatchers who aren’t told of the new feature will keep cross-checking against texts, which removes the efficiency gain.

Channels

1. Owned: A 10mins breakfast session before shift start session

2. Owned: In-app prompt that walks drivers through the first few confirmations”

3. Owned: Dispatcher text nudge

Enablement & Assets

1. Short walkthrough (on-site or virtual) to show drivers the one-action flow and address the “just in case the app dies” fear. Assets: Demo script, a 30-60 second demo video or GIF to replay in the call, a one-page quick guide

2. In-app prompt walking drivers through the workflow to guide the driver the first few times they confirm a stop, so the habit shifts at the moment of delivery. Assets: Step-by-step tooltip sequence, “skip” and “don’t show again” states, copy for each step

3. Dispatcher text nudge. Catches drivers at the moment they skip the app. Dispatcher replies to a “stop done” text with a short line pointing to the new checklist. Asset to build: 2 or 3 reply lines, one-page dispatcher briefing

Individual Insights

Where the work was hardest, the moment my thinking changed, and what I learned.

Friction points

One of the hardest friction point was understanding the numbers and trying to get a reasonable target metric to fit into the sprint. There were alot of sparing moments with Claude to work through this friction.
